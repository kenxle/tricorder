 Why do most VC candidates fail the investment memo? (What a great one actually looks like). 

 Here’s where most aspiring VCs actually get rejected.
 Not the screening call. Not “why VC.” You clear those. Then an email lands on a evening with a pitch deck attached and one line:
 “Take a look and send us a memo in next 48 hours. Whatever format you like.”
 No page limit. No structure. No rubric. You spend two days on it, hit send, and a week later get a polite no. Nobody ever tells you what was wrong with it.
 I’ve watched a lot of good people lose offers right here. And when I’ve read what they submitted, the problem was almost never intelligence. It was that they’d been asked to write a document they had never once seen an example of.
 What’s actually being tested 

 Candidates think the case study is testing whether they can pick a good company.
 It isn’t. The firm already has a view on that company - they’ve often already passed on it, invested in it, or invented it. Your yes or no barely matters.
 What’s being tested is: 
 Can you produce the artifact a junior investor produces, at a quality a partner wouldn’t be embarrassed to forward? 

 That’s the actual job. You won’t be picking anything for years. What you’ll be doing is turning messy, incomplete information into two pages a busy partner can act on in six minutes. Every week.
 So the question in their head isn’t “do I agree?” It’s “if I sent this round with your name on it, would I look good or careless?”

 Different question. Very different right answers.
 Why memos fail 

 The failures are remarkably consistent. Four patterns:
 Too long: Fourteen pages, because effort feels like it should count. It doesn’t - length reads as an inability to prioritise. 

 It’s a summary, not a judgement: The deck said the market is $13 billion, so the memo says the market is $13 billion. Nobody needed you for that. 

 Decorative risks: Competition, execution, market timing. Three risks that fit any company on earth. This is the loudest tell there is. 

 No recommendation: “Compelling opportunity with risks worth monitoring.” That’s not a decision. 

 None of these are intelligence problems. They’re format problems - which is good news, because format is learnable in an afternoon once someone shows you one.
 So today I’m showing you the actual thing.
