 Cultural Due Diligence: A Guide for Angel Investors

 By
 Brian Nichols

 Brian Nichols is the co-founder of Angel Squad , an angel investing community where aspiring and active angels learn from Hustle Fund’s approach, review curated deal flow, and connect with peers. 

 A polished pitch gives you little evidence of how founders handle a missed deadline, a disagreement, or an unhappy customer. Before you write a check, you need a view of how the team works. Cultural due diligence gives those conversations a purpose and helps you connect what you learn to the investment decision.

 What Is Cultural Due Diligence?

 Cultural due diligence is a structured assessment of a company’s working norms before an investment or transaction. It examines how people make decisions, share information, resolve disagreements, and respond to incentives. The evidence comes from behavior, working practices, and people who have seen the team in action.

 For an angel investor, the focus is a startup’s founders and early team. You’re assessing whether their habits support the company’s next milestones and what people-related risks you’d accept by investing.

 In mergers and acquisitions (M&A), the scope is different. A buyer assesses the cultures of two organizations and the challenges of combining them. Denison and Ko’s M&A research framework includes understanding both cultures and planning the transition. An angel investing in one startup usually has no integration plan to build.

 Cultural diligence also has a narrower focus than management due diligence . Management diligence examines leaders’ abilities, experience, and role coverage. Cultural diligence examines the habits they create across the team. A capable engineer can still lead a team where nobody feels able to report a product problem.

 Connect Culture to the Investment Case

 Start with what the company needs to do next. A startup selling to hospitals may need a dependable process for escalating safety concerns. A consumer app may need fast experiments and a clear way to stop ideas that users reject. Each company needs norms that suit its work.

 Our co-founder Elizabeth Yin’s pitch-question approach starts with the things a particular business has to get right. Apply that same discipline to culture. Name the behavior the investment case depends on, then gather evidence for it.

 For example, if growth requires the founders to recruit and delegate to a sales leader, ask how they’ve handed over decisions before. If the product needs constant customer learning, ask what happens when an employee’s findings contradict the CEO’s preferred direction.

 Culture findings sit alongside product, market, financial, and deal-term diligence. They don’t establish that a startup will succeed. Most of your startup bets may fail, including investments in thoughtful, well-run teams.

 Five Areas to Assess, With Questions and Evidence

 A values statement tells you what founders want the company to be. Recent examples show you how close they are to that ambition. For each area, ask about a specific event, who acted, and what happened afterward.

 1. Decision Ownership and Disagreement

 Clear ownership helps you understand how a team moves from disagreement to action. Founders can disagree strongly and still work well together. Pay attention to whether they can explain the decision and give a fair account of the other person’s reasoning.

 Ask:

- Who made the final call on your last contested product or hiring decision?

- What did each founder want, and what evidence changed the outcome?

- Which decisions can an employee make without asking a founder?

- Where do your responsibilities still overlap?

 Useful evidence includes a recent decision note, a working roadmap with named owners, and examples from colleagues who participated. In joint conversations, notice whether each founder can explain their own responsibilities. One person answering every question is a reason to ask more, not a verdict on the relationship.

 2. Bad News, Accountability, and Learning

 You want to know how an inconvenient fact reaches the person who can act on it. Ask for the sequence of a missed commitment or failed experiment, including what the leader did wrong.

 Ask:

- When did someone last bring you bad news, and what did you do first?

- Tell me about an employee who challenged your plan. What happened to their idea?

- What changed after your last missed deadline?

 Psychological safety means people believe they can take interpersonal risks, such as admitting an error or disagreeing. Amy Edmondson’s study of 51 teams in a manufacturing company found an association between psychological safety and learning behavior. It provides a reason to examine candor; it doesn’t establish a formula for startup returns.

 A useful example includes the mistake, its consequences, and a changed practice. A written review of the miss that names owners and follow-up actions carries more information than “we learn from failure.”

 3. Hiring, Expectations, and Incentives

 Hiring criteria and rewards show which behaviors the company encourages. Examine whether the founders’ promises to employees match the work they expect and the behavior they recognize.

 Ask:

- Why did your most recent hire choose to join?

- What would make a technically strong candidate wrong for this role?

- How do you explain workload, compensation, and uncertainty before someone accepts?

- What behavior earns praise? When has a high performer faced consequences for mistreating someone?

 Use role descriptions, an example of how expectations are communicated with private details removed, and employee accounts of joining the company. You don’t need private compensation details to ask whether expectations were clear.

 There’s no universal employee-equity percentage that proves a healthy culture. The relevant cultural question is whether promises are understandable and consistent with how people are treated. Detailed compensation and equity review belongs in the broader diligence process.

 4. Customer Treatment and Integrity

 Customer conversations can reveal whether internal values survive commercial pressure. Ask about a promise the startup struggled to keep, rather than requesting another happy-customer testimonial.

 Ask:

- What happened the last time you couldn’t deliver what you’d promised?

- Who told the customer, and when?

- Have you declined revenue because the request conflicted with a product or service commitment?

 A customer can describe how the team handled a delayed launch or unresolved issue. A support thread with private details removed can show who took ownership and whether the customer received a clear explanation. Keep the distinction between an ordinary execution mistake and knowingly misleading someone.

 5. Pressure and Operational Continuity

 Pressure creates tradeoffs. Look for how the team handles them, including whether it changes priorities without hiding the consequences from employees or customers.

 Ask:

- What did you stop doing when time or cash became tight?

- How did you communicate that change to the people affected?

- If a founder were unavailable for two weeks, who could handle essential customer, product, and payment responsibilities?

 The continuity question is about business coverage. Apply it consistently, without investigating anyone’s health, immigration status, marriage, or family arrangements.

 A small team may have limited backup today. Record the dependency and the founders’ practical plan for reducing it. A missing process at a startup’s earliest stage has a different meaning from the same gap in a team with several managers.

 Run a Proportionate Evidence-Gathering Process

 Start once the company clears your initial interest screen, before you commit capital. Keep the work focused on uncertainties that could change your decision.

 1. Choose Two or Three Culture Questions

 Write the beliefs your investment case depends on. For example, “The team can report product risks early enough to protect customer trust” is more useful than “The founders seem humble.”

 Set an initial scope with the founders. One focused founder conversation, a few relevant working documents, and two or three reference calls can be a manageable starting point. This is a suggested scope, not an industry benchmark. Expand it when a material concern remains unresolved.

 2. Gather Recent Examples and Working Documents

 Use documents the team already works with, such as a hiring brief, decision record, review of a missed goal, or customer-escalation process. Request versions with private details removed where appropriate. A two-person startup may explain these practices without having formal documentation.

 Speak with co-founders together and, where useful, separately. Use the same core questions. Separate conversations help clarify responsibilities and different perspectives; they shouldn’t become a trap for minor differences in wording.

 For a solo founder with no employees, use prior colleagues, collaborators, contractors, or customers who have seen relevant behavior. Mark the current team culture as largely unobserved. A strong account of past work cannot prove how future employees will experience the company.

 3. Add References With Different Vantage Points

 Explain your process to the founder and agree on appropriate outreach. Get permission before contacting current employees, employers, or customers. Avoid surprise outreach that could expose a confidential fundraise or disrupt someone’s work.

 Choose people who can address the specific uncertainty: a former direct report for delegation, a co-founder for conflict, or a customer for handling a broken promise. Include founder-provided references and other relevant contacts where appropriate.

 Ask each person how they know the founder, when they worked together, and which events they saw firsthand. Useful prompts include:

- What happened when someone disagreed with this person?

- Tell me about a commitment they missed. How did they handle it?

- What would need to change for you to work with them again?

 Founder-selected references may be unusually positive. Former employees may have grievances or limited context. Two people repeating the same secondhand story are one evidence trail. Look for behavior across distinct events and relationships.

 Public employee reviews can suggest questions, but anonymous posts and tiny samples carry limited weight. An employee departure also needs context: role, tenure, reason, company stage, and what changed afterward.

 4. Record the Evidence and Its Limits

 Keep observations separate from interpretations. “The employee described three launch decisions being reversed without explanation” is an observation from one source. “The CEO doesn’t trust the team” is an interpretation that needs more support.

 Write down competing explanations and missing evidence. Confidentiality can limit access for a legitimate reason. If a material question stays unanswered, record that uncertainty and decide whether you’re willing to accept it.

 Turn Culture Findings Into an Investment Decision

 Each finding needs a clear connection to the business. Record the claim, supporting evidence, potential consequence, and action. Avoid combining subjective impressions into a numerical “culture score” that looks more precise than your information allows.

 Our co-founder and general partner Elizabeth Yin writes in Democratizing Knowledge : “The more disciplined you are in your thought process/rubric, the more you can improve over time.” A short written record gives you a decision to revisit as the company develops.

 For each material finding, use five short entries:

- Observation: What happened, who observed it, and when?

- Business risk: Which milestone, customer relationship, or team responsibility could it affect?

- Counterevidence: What supports another explanation or shows the practice has changed?

- Confidence: Is the evidence corroborated, mixed, limited, or missing?

- Decision: Proceed with the risk understood, gather more evidence before committing, or pass.

 Consider an illustrative seed-stage team that says engineers can challenge launch dates. An engineer describes being overruled after flagging a defect. The founders acknowledge the incident and share a new release process with a named approval owner. A later customer account describes another launch delayed to fix a problem.

 Your conclusion could be that the original decision was poorly handled and there is some evidence of improvement. You’d still want to understand whether the new process is consistently used. That is a narrower, more useful conclusion than labeling the team “toxic” or “excellent.”

 In a second illustrative case, independent accounts describe repeated suppression of known defects and misleading customer promises. That would raise a material trust concern. We’d treat a substantiated pattern like that as a reason to pass, even if the pitch and growth looked attractive.

 Clear integrity concerns deserve more weight than a fixable gap in process. An unresolved ownership problem may warrant another founder conversation. A missing hiring document may need little weight at all. Any improvement you’re counting on needs a concrete plan and the founders’ commitment.

 Keep Personal Similarity Out of the Assessment

 Use the same core questions across companies, accept thoughtful written follow-ups, and record your observations before comparing impressions with other investors. Familiarity, charisma, an accent, or a shared employer tells you little about how someone treats colleagues.

 In Democratizing Knowledge , Elizabeth Yin notes, “Investors use their life perspective to assess.” Make your own assumptions visible. A quiet founder may handle disagreement well. An energetic founder may struggle to delegate. The evidence should carry the judgment.

 Culture will change as people join, roles shift, and the company encounters new problems. After investing, agreed updates and occasional conversations can show whether the risks you identified have changed. Support the founders without creating an intrusive reporting burden.
