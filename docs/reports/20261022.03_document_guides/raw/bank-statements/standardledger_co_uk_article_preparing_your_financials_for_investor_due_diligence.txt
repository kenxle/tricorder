 Due diligence can make or break an investment deal. Get your financials in top shape and ensure they’re investor-ready to breeze through the due diligence process.

 You’ve got investor interest. The pitch went well. Now comes due diligence – and this is where deals slow down, get restructured or quietly fall apart. Not because the business isn’t good, but because the financial house wasn’t in order when the scrutiny arrived.
 Financial due diligence is a thorough examination of your business’s financial records, structure and performance. Investors use it to verify that what you’ve told them in the pitch is accurate and that the risks they’re taking on are the ones they’ve priced in. The better prepared you are, the faster and smoother the process is – and the more confidence you project throughout.
 If you’d like help getting your financials into shape before a raise, talk to the team at Standard Ledger .

 Your Financial Statements Need to Be Clean and Current
 The three core documents investors will review are your profit and loss statement, your balance sheet and your cash flow statement. These aren’t just administrative outputs – they’re the primary evidence of how your business has actually performed, and investors will scrutinise them for consistency, accuracy and trend.
 For UK startups, this means more than having tidy management accounts. Your statutory accounts filed at Companies House need to be current – late or missing filings are a red flag before investors have even looked at the numbers. If you’re filing under FRS 102 or FRS 105, make sure your categorisation and presentation are consistent year-on-year so that trends are readable.
 Investors will typically want two to three years of financial statements where available, along with year-to-date management accounts for the current period. Any significant variance between your management accounts and your statutory accounts will require explanation, so reconcile those before the process begins rather than during it.
 Revenue Quality and Customer Metrics Matter as Much as the Top Line
 Investors aren’t just looking at your revenue figure – they’re looking at whether that revenue is sustainable, growing and driven by fundamentals rather than one-off events. For SaaS and subscription businesses this means having clean ARR, MRR, churn and net revenue retention figures you can substantiate. For other business models, it means a clear breakdown of revenue by product, channel or customer segment with the supporting data behind it.
 CAC and LTV will be examined carefully. A strong LTV-to-CAC ratio tells investors the business model is economically sound; a weak one – or an inability to explain the numbers – signals that growth is being bought at a cost that isn’t sustainable. Know your payback period and be ready to walk through the assumptions behind it.
 If a significant portion of your revenue comes from a small number of customers, be prepared to address concentration risk directly. That doesn’t mean it’s a deal-breaker, but investors will want to understand the nature of those relationships, their durability and what you’re doing to broaden the base.
 UK Tax and Regulatory Compliance Must Be Current
 HMRC compliance is one of the areas UK investors check specifically, and gaps here can cause significant delays. Before due diligence begins, make sure your Corporation Tax returns are filed and any outstanding liabilities are settled or formally arranged with HMRC. VAT returns need to be current and reconciled, and if you have employees, your PAYE and National Insurance records need to be in order.
 If you’ve made R&D tax credit claims, have the supporting documentation ready – project descriptions, cost breakdowns and any correspondence with HMRC. Investors will want to see that claims are well-evidenced and not at material risk of challenge.
 For startups that have raised under SEIS or EIS, compliance doesn’t end with advance assurance. Investors will check that you’ve maintained compliance with the conditions of the scheme – share structure, trading activity, use of funds – and that compliance certificates have been issued to investors. Any breach of SEIS or EIS conditions that hasn’t been addressed is a significant issue.
 Your Cap Table Needs to Tell a Clean Story
 Investors are buying into an ownership structure, and they need to understand exactly what they’re buying into before they commit. Your cap table should clearly show all founders, investors and employees with equity, including options and any convertible instruments, with the documentation to support each entry.
 For UK startups that have issued EMI options, make sure the HMRC valuation approval is in place and that grant notices have been properly issued and filed. Improperly documented EMI grants can lose their tax-advantaged status, which is a material issue for the employees holding them and a complication investors will want resolved before closing.
 Convertible loan notes and SAFEs should be clearly reflected in the cap table on a fully diluted basis, along with any discount rates, caps or pro-rata rights that will affect the next round. Investors want to see the full picture of how their investment will interact with existing instruments – not have it emerge during negotiation.
 Know Your Numbers Well Enough to Discuss Them Without Slides
 Due diligence conversations aren’t one-way document reviews. Investors will ask you questions, and your ability to answer them clearly – without needing to look things up – matters. Founders who know their gross margin, burn rate, runway, CAC and LTV instinctively come across as being in control of their business. Those who have to check notes or produce inconsistent figures from different versions of the same spreadsheet undermine confidence in the numbers themselves.
 Your runway calculation in particular needs to be watertight. Know exactly how many months of cash you have at current burn, what assumptions that’s based on, and what levers you could pull to extend it if you needed to. Investors will stress-test this, and having thought it through in advance – including the uncomfortable scenarios – is part of what you’re demonstrating in due diligence.
 Getting Ready Before You Start
 The mistake most founders make is treating due diligence preparation as something that happens after investor interest arrives. By that point, you’re rushing through reconciliations and document gathering under time pressure while simultaneously managing investor conversations. Starting the process well in advance – with clean books, a current cap table and organised compliance documentation – means you can move quickly when the interest is there.

 FAQ
 Questions founders ask us.
 These are the things founders most often want to know before working with us. If your question isn't here, bring it along when you speak with us.

 What do UK investors look at during financial due diligence? 
 Investors will work through your financial statements, revenue and customer metrics, tax compliance records, cap table and your ability to discuss key metrics clearly. For UK startups specifically, they’ll also check Companies House filings are current, HMRC obligations are met and – if you’ve raised under SEIS or EIS – that SEIS and EIS compliance is in order. The more organised and accurate your records are before the process begins, the faster and smoother due diligence tends to be.

 How far back do investors typically want to see financial records? 
 Two to three years of financial statements is standard where available, plus year-to-date management accounts for the current period. If your business is younger than that, you’ll provide what you have. Investors will also look for consistency between your statutory accounts filed at Companies House and the management accounts you’ve been presenting in conversations – significant variances will need a clear explanation.

 Does my SEIS or EIS compliance matter during due diligence? 
 Yes – and it’s often underestimated. Investors will check that advance assurance was obtained, that the conditions of the scheme have been maintained and that compliance certificates have been issued to qualifying investors. A breach of SEIS or EIS conditions – even an inadvertent one – is a material complication that will need to be resolved before a deal closes. If you’re uncertain about your compliance position, it’s worth reviewing this before you start investor conversations.

 What should my cap table look like before investor due diligence? 
 It should be fully up to date on a diluted basis, with all founders, investors and employees clearly listed alongside their equity or options. Every entry should have supporting documentation – share certificates, option grant notices, convertible loan note agreements. For EMI options specifically, HMRC valuation approval and properly issued grant notices need to be in place. A messy or incomplete cap table is one of the most common causes of delays in UK deal processes.

 When should I start preparing for investor due diligence? 
 Well before you start investor conversations – not after interest arrives. By the time a term sheet is on the table, you want your books reconciled, your tax compliance current, your cap table documented and your key metrics ready to discuss without needing to look anything up. Preparing under time pressure while managing active investor conversations is stressful and risks mistakes. We’d always recommend treating due diligence readiness as an ongoing standard, not a pre-raise sprint.
