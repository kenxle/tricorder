 Startup Accounting Mistakes That Can Cause Problems During Fundraising Due Diligence

 by

 Bryan Long, MBA 

 Content Marketing Manager

 Published: August 20, 2026 

 Startup accounting is the process of recording, reconciling, and reporting a company’s financial activity so founders, investors, and other stakeholders can rely on the numbers. During fundraising due diligence, investors use startup accounting records to evaluate a company’s cash position, spending, revenue, liabilities, financial controls, and use of prior investor capital.

 For venture-backed companies, accurate accounting for startups helps confirm that the financial information in the pitch deck, data room, board materials, and management discussions is accurate and consistent. Investors may request financial statements, bank and credit-card records, tax filings, payroll reports, customer contracts, budgets, cap-table information, and support for major transactions.

 A startup does not need enterprise-level finance operations to be diligence-ready. But it should be able to provide timely, reconciled financials and clearly explain the story behind the numbers. When startup bookkeeping is incomplete or outdated, founders may spend valuable fundraising time recreating records, resolving discrepancies, and answering preventable questions about cash runway, revenue, expenses, and financial controls.

 Why Accounting Matters in Due Diligence

 During a fundraising round , investors often review much more than a pitch deck and headline metrics. They may request financial statements, bank records, tax filings, payroll reports, customer contracts, cap-table information, budgets, and details behind major expenses or revenue balances.

 The goal is usually not to expect a young company to have enterprise-level finance operations. Investors understand that early-stage startups are still building. What matters is whether the company can provide credible, consistent information and explain the story behind the numbers.

 When startup bookkeeping is incomplete, founders can lose valuable time reconstructing transactions, tracking down documents, and correcting historical reports. In more serious cases, accounting issues can create questions about cash runway, revenue quality, compliance, internal controls, or the use of investor capital.

 Common Startup Accounting Mistakes

 1. Books that are not closed or reconciled

 One of the most common due diligence issues is simply having books that are behind. A startup may have transactions recorded in its accounting system, but bank accounts, corporate cards, payment processors, and other accounts have not been reconciled.

 A reconciliation compares the company’s accounting records with actual bank, card, payroll, or processor activity. Without it, the reported cash balance, expenses, liabilities, and revenue may not be accurate.

 For example, a founder may report $1.2 million in available cash based on the bank balance. But if unpaid bills, credit-card balances, pending payroll items, or customer refunds have not been accounted for, the company’s true cash position and runway may look different.

 What to do: Reconcile every cash, credit-card, and payment-processing account every month. Complete a monthly close that produces a finalized income statement , balance sheet , and cash flow statement .

 2. Personal and company expenses are mixed together

 In the earliest days of a company, founders sometimes pay business costs on personal cards or use company funds for personal expenses. Occasional founder-paid business expenses are manageable when documented and reimbursed correctly. Ongoing commingling is not.

 Mixed expenses create several problems during accounting for startups:

- The company’s expense reporting becomes less reliable.

- The accounting team may not have support for the transaction.

- Reimbursements or amounts owed to founders may be unclear.

- Investors may question financial controls and cash management.

- Tax reporting can become more complicated.

 What to do: Use dedicated company bank accounts and corporate cards . Establish a straightforward reimbursement process, require receipts, and record founder-paid expenses consistently.

 3. Weak documentation for major transactions

 A financial statement shows the accounting result of a transaction. Due diligence also requires the source documents that explain it.

 Investors may ask for documentation related to large vendor payments, executive compensation, customer agreements, debt, leases, financing transactions, equity grants, and unusual expenses. If those documents are scattered across personal inboxes, old Slack threads, or employee laptops, the diligence process becomes slower and more difficult.

 What to do: Create a centralized, secure repository for contracts, invoices, bank statements, financing documents, payroll reports, board approvals, and tax filings. Use a consistent naming convention so the team can find documents quickly.

 4. Revenue is recorded incorrectly

 Revenue accounting can become complicated earlier than founders expect, especially for SaaS companies, annual contracts, prepaid subscriptions, implementation fees, usage-based pricing, or contracts with multiple deliverables.

 A common mistake is recording the full value of a customer contract as revenue when the cash arrives or the invoice is issued (cash accounting). In many cases, the company may need to recognize revenue over the period it provides the product or service ( accrual accounting ).

 For example, if a customer pays $24,000 upfront for a 12-month subscription, treating the full amount as revenue in the first month can overstate near-term performance. The accounting records may need to recognize the revenue over the subscription period (in other words, $2,000 per month) while recording the unearned portion as deferred revenue.

 What to do: Maintain customer contracts, invoices, billing schedules, and a revenue-recognition schedule. Review the treatment of nonstandard terms, annual prepayments, discounts, refunds, and professional-services obligations.

 5. Expenses are poorly categorized

 Founders and investors need to understand where money is going. If the chart of accounts groups most spending into broad labels such as “software,” “consulting,” or “other expense,” it can be hard to evaluate burn, hiring costs, customer acquisition spending, and gross margin.

 Poor categorization can also lead to inconsistent financial reporting. One month, an engineering contractor may be classified as research and development; the next month, it may be put into general and administrative expense. That makes trend analysis less useful.

 What to do: Use a practical chart of accounts that separates meaningful business functions, such as research and development, sales and marketing, general and administrative, cost of revenue, payroll, contractors, legal, and recruiting. Apply the same classification rules each month.

 6. Payroll, contractor, and equity records do not match

 Payroll is often a company’s largest expense, which means discrepancies are easy for investors to spot. Problems can arise when payroll reports do not match the general ledger, contractor payments are not documented, or headcount reporting differs from financial statements.

 Equity records can create similar issues. A cap table that does not align with financing documents, board approvals, stock-option grants, or accounting records can slow the diligence process significantly.

 What to do: Reconcile payroll reports to the general ledger each month, retain signed contractor agreements, and keep the company’s cap-table platform, financing records, and board approvals current. Coordinate regularly with legal, HR, payroll, and accounting partners.

 7. No clear view of burn rate and runway

 A founder should be able to explain current cash, monthly burn, and expected runway without relying on an estimate created the night before an investor meeting.

 Burn and runway calculations can vary depending on whether the company uses gross burn, net burn, or a forecast that incorporates expected revenue and hiring. The key is not to present a single “perfect” number. It is to use a consistent methodology, tie it back to current financials, and explain the assumptions.

 What to do: Review cash and burn regularly, maintain a budget or forecast, and document the assumptions behind hiring plans, revenue expectations, and major expenditures. Update the forecast as the company’s plans change.

 Startup Accounting Basics for a Diligence-Ready Company

 The best time to prepare for fundraising due diligence is before fundraising begins. Strong startup accounting basics create a repeatable process, not a last-minute cleanup project.

 A diligence-ready startup should generally have:

- Monthly reconciled bank, credit-card, payroll, and payment-processor accounts

- Timely monthly financial statements

- A consistent chart of accounts

- Clear revenue, billing, and deferred-revenue records, when applicable

- Organized support for major expenses and contracts

- Current payroll, contractor, and headcount information

- Updated financing, cap-table, and equity documentation

- Filed tax returns and an organized record of tax notices and filings

- A budget, cash forecast, and clear burn-rate calculation

- A secure data room or shared folder for finance and legal documents

 These practices benefit more than a future fundraising round. They help leadership make better operating decisions, give boards more useful reporting, and reduce surprises around cash, compliance, and spending.

 What do investors review during startup fundraising due diligence?

 Investors often request historical financial statements, bank statements, tax returns, payroll reports, customer contracts, accounts receivable and payable details, budgets, cash forecasts, financing documents, and cap-table records. The specific request list depends on the company’s stage, business model, and size of the round. To find out more about the diligence requirements for different startup stages, see our diligence guide .

 How far back should startup financial records go for due diligence?

 Investors commonly want to review the company’s recent financial history, along with supporting records for material transactions and financing events. Startups should keep organized accounting records from incorporation onward, including bank statements, tax filings, equity records, significant contracts, and monthly financial statements.

 Can a startup raise funding if its books are behind?

 Yes, but delayed or incomplete books can slow diligence and create more questions from investors. If records are behind, founders should address the issue early, complete reconciliations, identify any cleanup needs, and ensure financial statements accurately reflect the company’s current position before sharing them with prospective investors.

 When should a startup outsource accounting before fundraising?

 Consider outsourcing when the company has raised capital, added employees, manages recurring or complex revenue, needs monthly investor reporting, or lacks a reliable view of cash and runway. An experienced startup accounting firm can help prepare diligence-ready financials while establishing processes that scale beyond the next fundraising round.
